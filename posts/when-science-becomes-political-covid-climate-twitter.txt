## The Broader Context: Science, Politics, and Social Media

We live in "knowledge societies" where science and politics constantly interact. Public health advice, climate policy, vaccination campaigns — all rely on scientific evidence. But when science enters political debate, it also becomes *politicized*.

Not all politicization is bad. In a democracy, some degree of contestation is necessary for science‑informed policymaking and public debate.

But where is the line?

- When does legitimate critique of science‑informed policy turn into **ideological science rejection**?
- How can we distinguish healthy democratic debate from **disinformation and misinformation**?

During the COVID‑19 pandemic, these questions became urgent. Public discourse about masks, vaccines, and lockdowns was everywhere. And interestingly, much of that discourse became **discursively linked** with another already‑politicized science issue: **climate change**.

The research team (including a collaboration with Utrecht University) wanted to understand this phenomenon systematically.

## The Scientific Problem

The study investigates how scientific knowledge is politicized on Twitter. Specifically, it asks:

- What are the **discursive modes** of online politicization?
- How do these modes relate to different **online issue publics** (commun organised around specific topics)?
- And crucially: when does politicization become *problematic* in a democratic context?

The research distinguishes between:
| Type | Description |
|------|-------------|
| **Politicization that contests science‑informed policy** | Legitimate democratic debate |
| **Ideological science rejection** | Dismissing science itself, not just policy |

This framing moves beyond the simplistic "information vs. disinformation" binary — a more nuanced and, for the social sciences, more useful approach.

## The Methodological Challenge

To answer these questions empirically, the team needed a **large, structured dataset** of Twitter discourse covering both COVID‑19 and climate change — and crucially, **tweets that explicitly linked the two issues**.

**Why this is difficult:**

| Challenge | Why it matters |
|-----------|----------------|
| **Scale** | Thousands of relevant posts, not a handful |
| **Noise** | Most tweets are irrelevant, spam, or off‑topic |
| **Linking detection** | Identifying tweets that connect COVID‑19 AND climate change requires more than simple keywords |
| **Time sensitivity** | Discourse evolves; collection had to cover specific windows |
| **Ethics & platform limits** | API restrictions, rate limits, terms of service |
| **Manual alternative** | A researcher manually searching and copying would take **months** — and still miss patterns |

The alternative — manual collection and coding — would not only be slow but also introduce bias and limit replicability.

## The Methodological Solution (What Danalytica Built)

We designed and implemented an end‑to‑end data engineering pipeline tailored to the research question.

### Data Gathering

Using Twitter API (respecting academic track terms), we:

- **Identified relevant keywords and hashtags:** covering COVID‑19, climate change, and their intersection.
- **Collected tweets over defined time windows:** aligned with key political and scientific events.
- **Captured rich metadata:** author, timestamp, retweet/quote/like counts, thread context, and reply networks.

The raw dataset ran into tens of thousands of tweets — far beyond what any manual effort could achieve.

### Structuring & Cleaning

Raw Twitter data is notoriously messy. We built a cleaning pipeline to:

- Remove duplicates, retweets, and near‑duplicates (reducing noise without losing conversational context).
- Filter spam and clearly irrelevant content using rule‑based heuristics.
- Normalise text (consistent casing, URL/mention handling, emoji preservation).
- Extract entities (user mentions, hashtags, URLs) as separate fields for network analysis.
- Flag language to allow later stratification.

### ML Classification for Thematic Sorting

The core analytical task required classifying tweets not by simple keywords, but by **discursive mode** — e.g., "contesting policy" vs. "rejecting science itself," and identifying tweets that explicitly **linked COVID‑19 and climate change**.

We trained a supervised classifier using:

- A small, manually labelled seed set provided by the researcher (based on qualitative reading of a few hundred tweets).
- TF‑IDF features plus lightweight embeddings (fast, interpretable, and sufficient for this task).
- **Active learning:** the model flagged uncertain cases; the researcher labelled a small batch; the model retrained — repeated over five cycles.

The final classifier achieved **reliable accuracy (>85%)** on a held‑out test set, allowing the research team to:

- Categorise the entire dataset of tens of thousands of tweets in **minutes**, not months.
- Identify which tweets linked COVID‑19 and climate change, and classify their political mode.
- Produce replicable, transparent results — every step documented.

## What Was Achieved

With a clean, structured, and categorised dataset, the research team could:

- **Identify different discursive modes of politicization** — showing that some critiques target policy while others reject science itself.
- **Map how these modes relate to different online issue publics** (communities organised around climate vs. COVID vs. politics).
- **Provide empirical evidence** for when politicization of science becomes problematic in a democratic context.
- **Move beyond the "information vs. disinformation" binary** — offering a more nuanced framework for future research.

The alternative — manual collection and thematic coding — would have taken an estimated **3–4 months** of a PhD researcher's time. Our pipeline reduced that to **under three weeks**, including iteration and refinement.

> *More importantly, it freed the researchers to focus on interpretation, theory building, and public debate — not wrestling with API rate limits and deduplication logic.*

## Key Takeaways for Research Teams

If you are an academic working with social media data, here are actionable lessons from this collaboration:

| Lesson | Why it matters |
|--------|----------------|
| **Start with a small qualitative sample** | It helps you define categories before scaling up. |
| **Use active learning** | You don't need massive labelled datasets; a few hundred seed examples + iterative refinement can reach good accuracy. |
| **Build a cleaning pipeline before scaling** | Dirty data at scale is paralyzing — clean as you go. |
| **Document every transformation** | Replicability is the currency of good science. |
| **Partner with engineers early** | A week of engineering can save months of manual labour — and enable analysis that would otherwise be impossible. |
